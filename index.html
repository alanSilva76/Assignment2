Mexican Patriotic Month September

Celebrating Mexican Independence and the rich history, vibrant culture, and remarkable gastronomy that make Mexico truly unique.

In Mexico, September is celebrated as the Mexican Patriotic Month, a time to honor the country's history, culture, and independence. The month is marked by various festivities, including parades, traditional music and dance performances, and the display of the Mexican flag. It is a period of national pride and reflection on the values that define the nation.

One of the most significant events during this month is the celebration of Mexican Independence Day on September 16th. This day commemorates the start of the Mexican War of Independence from Spanish rule in 1810. The festivities often include reenactments of historical events, speeches by public officials, and community gatherings.

Independence of Mexico

I. The Colonial Foundations of New Spain

The Independence of Mexico was a profound historical, political, and social process through which the territory of New Spain broke away from nearly three centuries of Spanish colonial rule. The movement was shaped by growing political, economic, and social tensions within colonial society, as well as by broader intellectual currents that questioned traditional systems of authority and privilege. For much of the colonial period, political power and economic opportunities were concentrated in the hands of a relatively small portion of society. The Spanish-born elite occupied many of the highest governmental and ecclesiastical positions, while people born in New Spain, indigenous communities, mestizos, and other groups faced significant social and political limitations.

II. The Causes of Independence

The causes of the independence movement were complex and developed over many years. Among the principal factors were social inequality, political restrictions, economic exploitation, and dissatisfaction with colonial administration. The Spanish Crown maintained extensive control over the economy of New Spain, including trade, taxation, and the exploitation of its abundant natural resources. At the same time, reforms implemented by the Spanish monarchy during the eighteenth century increased royal authority and placed additional economic and administrative burdens on the population. The spread of ideas concerning liberty, equality, popular sovereignty, and individual rights also influenced the emerging independence movement. The American Revolution and the French Revolution, together with the political crisis experienced by Spain following the Napoleonic invasion, created an environment in which the possibility of political change became increasingly conceivable.

III. The Cry of Dolores: The Beginning of the Revolution

The struggle formally began on September 16, 1810, when the priest Miguel Hidalgo y Costilla called upon the population of Dolores to rise against colonial authority. This historic event became known as the Grito de Dolores, or Cry of Dolores, and is traditionally regarded as the beginning of Mexico's War of Independence. Hidalgo's call rapidly attracted thousands of supporters, including indigenous people, mestizos, farmers, and members of other sectors of colonial society. What began as a regional uprising quickly developed into a large-scale revolutionary movement. Although Hidalgo's forces achieved important early victories, the insurgent army ultimately suffered military defeats. Hidalgo was captured and executed in 1811, but his death did not bring the movement to an end.

IV. José María Morelos and the Vision of a Nation

Following Hidalgo's death, José María Morelos y Pavón emerged as one of the most important leaders of the independence movement. Under his leadership, the struggle acquired a more organized political character. Morelos advocated the creation of an independent nation founded upon principles of sovereignty, equality, and the abolition of social privileges. In 1813, representatives of the insurgent movement convened at the Congress of Chilpancingo, where Morelos presented his influential document, Sentiments of the Nation (Sentimientos de la Nación). This document expressed many of the political ideals that would shape the future Mexican nation, including independence from Spain and the principle that sovereignty should reside in the people. Morelos was captured and executed in 1815, marking another major setback for the insurgency. Nevertheless, the struggle for independence continued.

V. The Long Struggle for Independence

After the death of Morelos, the independence movement entered a more fragmented phase. Numerous insurgent groups continued fighting against Spanish forces, particularly in regions where resistance remained strong. Among the most persistent leaders was Vicente Guerrero, who maintained the insurgent cause in southern Mexico despite difficult military and political circumstances. For several years, the conflict continued without a decisive victory for either side. Spain retained control over much of the territory, while insurgent forces continued to resist colonial authority.

VI. The Final Alliance: Iturbide and Guerrero

A significant turning point occurred in 1820, when political developments in Spain weakened the position of the colonial authorities in New Spain. These circumstances opened the possibility for negotiations between groups that had previously been enemies. Agustín de Iturbide, a former royalist military commander, joined forces with insurgent leader Vicente Guerrero. Their alliance resulted in the Plan of Iguala, proclaimed in February 1821. The plan promoted three fundamental principles, commonly known as the Three Guarantees: religion, independence, and unity. This political and military alliance led to the creation of the Army of the Three Guarantees, or Trigarante Army, which rapidly gained support throughout New Spain.

VII. The Consumation of Independence

The struggle that had begun eleven years earlier reached its decisive conclusion in 1821. On September 27, 1821, the Trigarante Army entered Mexico City, marking the effective end of Spanish colonial rule. The following day, the Declaration of Independence of the Mexican Empire was signed, formally proclaiming Mexico's independence from Spain. After nearly three centuries of colonial rule and eleven years of armed conflict, Mexico had emerged as an independent nation.

VIII. The Birth of a New Nation

The Independence of Mexico represented far more than the political separation of a colony from the Spanish Empire. It marked the beginning of a new historical era and the difficult process of constructing a sovereign nation. The ideals of freedom, sovereignty, equality, and national identity became central to the political development of Mexico. However, independence did not immediately eliminate the social and economic inequalities that had characterized colonial society. The newly independent nation faced enormous challenges, including political instability, economic difficulties, debates over the form of government, and the task of defining what it meant to be Mexican.

IX. A Legacy That Shaped Mexico

The events of 1810–1821 permanently transformed the history of Mexico. The sacrifices of figures such as Miguel Hidalgo y Costilla, José María Morelos y Pavón, Ignacio Allende, Vicente Guerrero, and many others became fundamental to the country's historical memory. The Independence movement established the foundation upon which modern Mexico would gradually be built. Its legacy continues to be commemorated every September 16, Mexico's Independence Day, honoring the beginning of a struggle that ultimately transformed New Spain into an independent nation.
